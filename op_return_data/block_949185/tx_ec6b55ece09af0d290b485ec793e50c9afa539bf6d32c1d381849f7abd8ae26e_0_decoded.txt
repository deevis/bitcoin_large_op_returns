The Arch of Titus, constructed in 81 CE, commemorates the Roman triumph awarded to Emperor Vespasian and his son, Titus, for their victory in the First Jewish-Roman War. Erected just southeast of the Roman Forum, the monument famously contains a panel of Roman soldiers carrying spoils, including the seven-branched menorah, gold trumpets, and the table of showbread, from the Temple in Jerusalem. The monument stands as a symbol of Jewish diaspora and Roman conquest, yet what caused the war to begin? When Roman procurator Gessius Florus seized funds from the Temple treasury in Jerusalem and massacred civilians, the slowly mounting tensions following the Roman annexation of Judea finally erupted, launching a full-scale Jewish revolt in 66 A.D. The war ended in 73 A.D., with a decisive Roman victory at the fortress of Masada, and the destruction of Jerusalem and the Second Temple in its wake.  The war was documented by Flavius Josephus, a Jewish–Roman military leader, priest, historian, and controversial figure in Jewish antiquity, in his work The Jewish War. His reputation stems from his defection to Rome in 67 A.D., during the war, after which he wrote under imperial patronage. This raises the imperative question of: can his work, in light of his reputation, still be considered a Jewish text?  Although The Jewish War does not critique the Jewish religion, its condemnation of the actions committed by those who participated in the revolt, as this analysis will argue, decisively make the text not Jewish. Throughout the seven-book work, Josephus frames Jewish sufferance as divinely ordained, portrays revolting Jews as unworthy of God’s favor, casts the revolt itself as anti-Jewish, and positions himself as a prophetic figure. 	In chapter 8 of Book II Josephus proclaims that, “No one obtains the government without God’s assistance,” framing Roman authority as divinely sanctioned (7). Thus, God is subsequently positioned against the Jewish revolt, and the Jews, in turn, are positioned against divine rule. Yet, as the war showed, the Jews were not even worthy of God’s favor. During the siege of the Second Temple, he depicted the Jews as cowardly, helpless, and unable.  The Second Temple was a sacred space to the Jewish relationship with God. Yet, in chapter 3 of Book II, Josephus describes the Roman siege, in which, upon being overwhelmed, some Jews, defending the temple, decided to take their own lives to avoid being killed by the Romans (3). Thus, because the Jews were unable and unwilling to defend the spoils of God, they were subsequently deemed by Josephus unworthy of God’s favor.  	In this regard, The Jewish War frames the Jewish revolt as catalyst for a war with God against the entire Jewish community, which was involuntarily caught in the conflict, forced to deal with collateral damage. In describing the Jewish people’s relationship with God, Josephus characterizes them as both unholy and unworthy. Yet, his critiques do not end there, as he goes on to divide the religion based on the revolt.  Josephus’s criticisms of the Jewish revolt work to redefine legitimate Judaism as compatible with Roman rule. In chapter 8 of Book II, Josephus discusses Judas, the teacher of a particular sect of Judaism, who was described as “not at all like the rest” (1). Because Judas’s sect was largely influential in the revolt, Josephus isolated it from the rest of Judaism, an otherwise wise and refined religion.  	In this regard, to be considered Jewish, one seemingly had to accept Roman authority. This is emphasized through his critique of Simon bar Giora, a key figure in the revolt. In chapter 4 of Book IV, Josephus refers to him as a tyrant (1). In chapter 18 of Book II, he calls Simon mischievous and destructive to the Jewish community. Even making note of how Simon kills other Jews willingly and frequently (4).  	Thus, if you are to be a part of the rebellion, you are not aligning yourself with the Jewish populace. Instead, you are going outside the boundaries of legitimate Judaism and into dangerous and violent factions. In this framework, Josephus suggests that resistance to Rome is not only misguided, but ultimately detrimental to the Jewish people. Yet, what actually makes his criticisms so poignant is his declaration of himself as a prophetic figure.  In chapter 8 of Book III, Josephus asserts that he is a prophetic figure. In the chapter, he describes being trapped with Jewish rebels, after invoking divine guidance, is compelled to surrender under the prophecy that Vespasian would become emperor (3-9). Thus, his words become an extension of God’s will, meaning his warnings against rebellion are framed as divinely sanctioned. Furthermore, resistance can be seen as futile.  	By advising against participation in the rebellion, Josephus implies that the revolt is destined to fail. Through his critiques, he repeatedly frames the rebels as acting against God’s will, whether by deviating from the Jewish religion’s reputation or by depicting them as overwhelmed when tested by what he presents as God’s instrument. This framing transforms political resistance into theological error, reinforcing Josephus’s claim to interpret divine will.  Yet, classifying this text as not Jewish is not an easy classification to make. This is because Josephus does not shy away from depicting the Roman difficulties faced during the war. In chapter 19 of Book II, Cestius Gallus,  the Roman governor of Syria, actually failed to besiege Jerusalem, causing him to retreat (7). However, this was not made to glorify the Jewish resistance, but instead, to emphasize and celebrate the Roman victory. Josephus likely included this to elevate Roman stature. By constructing the war as one of epic proportions, he reinforced the appearance of divine favor. God did not subject his army to an easy test of endurance, yet they ultimately emerged victorious regardless. An easy war would not be a compelling one to write about. If God allowed Rome to win despite hardship, then Rome deserved to rule. In conclusion, The Jewish War should not be considered a Jewish text, as it presents a deceptivley biased account of the First Jewish–Roman War that aligns skewed religious interpretations with Roman authority. The text is far too critical of what, given the circumstances, was a reasonable revolt. But, unfortunately, one of the most well-documented accounts of the war is shaped by the author’s choices and personal allegiances.
